v3 — the categorical tier

The axis (blue / rose) and its ramps are settled. This is the other half: the hues that answer “which one is this” — teal, violet, orange, yellow — measured as a working set for the first time rather than one at a time.

1 · What is actually at stake

Nothing consumes these yet

Every app in code/ was grepped for --chart-1 through --chart-7. Slots 1, 4, 5, 6 and 7 appear in zero consumer files. Slots 2 and 3 appear in exactly one — ccd-brb-onboarding/app/globals.css — where they are borrowed to build a diverging Likert ramp.

So the categorical tier has never once been used categorically, and the only real use of it was doing the axis’s job before the axis existed. Changing it breaks nothing. That is worth knowing before defending any of it.

3 · Why it cannot simply be fixed by picking better hues

The wheel is full, and the lightness window is narrow

Two constraints close in from opposite directions.

The hue constraint

Status and the axis already occupy fixed positions. Anything within about 35° of one of these, at a similar lightness, lands under ΔE 10.

The lightness constraint

A chart fill has to work on a white card and on the dark card (#212327), because one palette serves both modes. Requiring 3:1 on each traps every categorical colour in a 0.15-wide band of OKLCh lightness.

Search the whole space — every lightness from 0.40 to 0.80, every hue in 5° steps, at the highest chroma that stays in gamut — and keep only colours that clear 3:1 on both grounds and sit ΔE 12 or more from every status and axis colour. 69 colours survive out of thousands, in three arcs: ~90–120° (olive), ~165–215° (teal), ~305–345° (purple).

Best set of N, chosen to maximise the worst pairworst ΔEVerdict
2 hues — teal + purple23.5Comfortable. Both clear every fixed colour by 14+.
3 hues — teal + purple + olive14.1Works, but every pair is near the floor and olive is the only third that exists.
4 hues—No set of four clears the floor. The fourth always lands on status or on the axis.

Splitting into separate light-mode and dark-mode values was tested too, on the theory that each mode alone has a wider window. It buys 1.1 ΔE on the best triple and doubles what has to be maintained. Not worth it.

The pattern is not that ten is impossible — it is that ten is a choice to spend contrast on count. Tableau, d3 and Observable all did it, and all ship palettes where half the series cannot hold a fill on white. Carbon chose the other side: five, all readable, all far apart. Carbon is the model here, because CCD charts are read on white cards inside apps, not on the neutral grounds those three assume.

6 · Colour blindness — the one place option B wins

Teal and purple collapse into each other; olive does not

Simulated with the Machado 2009 matrices at full severity. ~8% of men have one of these, so on an internal audience of a few hundred this is a handful of real readers.

The line that matters most is the first: the axis survives. Everything that carries meaning — better/worse, agree/disagree — stays readable, which is the right thing to have protected.

Teal↔purple at 6.3 is a genuine hole in option A. Adding olive does not close it — it adds a third series that everyone can tell apart, while the first two still merge. Searching for a pair that clears colour blindness and both contrast floors returns purple + olive and drops teal entirely: worst separation 14.0, and no teal in the palette at all.

7 · Recommendation

A — two hues and grey

Take A. Teal #009E98, purple #AD65BE, grey #C6C8CC. Drop orange and yellow outright rather than leaving them deferred.

Three reasons. The data does not need three. Company is 27 categories — colour was never going to carry that; wave and tier are ordinal, so they belong to the sequential ramp; the genuine 3-category case is respondent role, and there the third value is usually a residual that grey already says better than a hue does.

The third hue costs more than it returns. Olive is the only colour on the wheel that fits, it lands ΔE 14.1 from the positive dot and 14.2 from caution-strong, and it sits visually between “good” green and “warning” yellow — a reader will look for meaning in it.

Two is honest about what the palette is. Seven slots where five are unusable is worse than two slots that work, because the seven invite a chart nobody can read.

What A implies, written as a rule: a chart with more than two nominal categories does not get more colours — it gets a different chart. Small multiples, or one hue plus grey with the subject named in the title, or a sorted bar chart in a single hue where position carries the identity. That is already the house rule (“most charts want one hue”); this just stops the palette from pretending otherwise.

Open, and not solved by either option: teal and purple merge for a deuteranopic reader. Mitigated the same way it always is — colour is never the only channel, so a two-series chart needs its series labelled directly rather than through a legend. The axis, which is where meaning actually lives, is unaffected.
